<h2>Has Spielberg used AI since SXSW?</h2>
<p>Not on a film, by his own account, and in May he said where the line sits. On the IMO podcast with Michelle Obama and Craig Robinson, he said: "Use AI as a tool, but do not use AI as the final word on anything creative. That's where I draw the line." He added that AI could "save us a lot of legwork" on tasks like scouting locations, and that "I don't believe there is any substitute for the soul", as <a href="https://au.variety.com/2026/film/global/steven-spielberg-hollywood-ai-final-word-creative-37059/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Variety</a> reported on May 28.</p>
<p>That matches what he said in Austin in March. At SXSW he told the audience that in his writers' rooms "all the seats are occupied" and that "I am not for AI if it replaces a creative individual", according to <a href="https://decrypt.co/361207/steven-spielberg-is-not-for-ai-replacing-creatives" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Decrypt</a>. So the "yet" in his famous sentence was never a door left open for a generated film. It is a director saying the tool can carry equipment, and cannot sit at the table.</p>
<p>He did not publish a checklist. Those two interviews do turn into one, short enough to run on your own production before a job starts:</p>
<pre><code>THE EMPTY CHAIR CHECK (from Spielberg's two 2026 interviews)
For each place AI enters the job, answer in one line:
1. Is it doing legwork (scouting, sorting, transcribing, previs)
   or is it making a creative decision?
2. Whose chair would be empty if this tool were not here?
3. Who has the final word on this output, by name?
4. Did that person see it before the client did?
5. If the client asks "who made this choice", is the answer a person?
Any "nobody" or "the tool" in lines 3 to 5: stop and assign a person.
</code></pre>
<p>Where the legwork sits in a working pipeline is laid out in <a href="/blog/higgsfield-ai-tutorial-how-a-director-uses-it-on-real-jobs/">how a director uses Higgsfield on real jobs</a>, and the same question about authorship is the one the Academy wrote into <a href="/blog/oscars-ai-rules-human-authorship-filmmakers-future/">its rules on human authorship</a>.</p>

<h2>When a Legend Draws a Line</h2>
<p>On March 13, at SXSW 2026, Steven Spielberg said something that immediately circulated through every filmmaker forum and group chat: "I've never used AI on any of my films yet."</p>
<p>That "yet" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. But the statement landed as a kind of moral flag: one of the greatest filmmakers alive publicly distancing himself from the thing the industry can't stop talking about.</p>
<p>Two weeks later, a festival built on refusing the tools was due back in Hollywood. Justine Bateman's Credo 23 Film Festival was first held in March 2025 at American Legion Post 43, where filmmakers signed a pledge that no generative AI was used on their film, as <a href="https://www.thewrap.com/non-ai-film-festival-justine-bateman-credo-23" target="_blank" rel="noopener">TheWrap</a> reported at the time. A second edition was announced for March 27, 2026, at the same venue. The premise: films made without AI tools, as a statement about craft and authenticity.</p>
<p>I understand the impulse. I also think the conversation is more tangled than the headlines suggest.</p>

<h2>What Spielberg Is Saying</h2>
<p>I want to take Spielberg at his word and think about what the statement really means.</p>
<p>He said he hasn't used AI <em>yet</em>. That "yet" suggests he's watching, not dismissing. A filmmaker who spent decades pushing visual effects technology (who co-founded DreamWorks during the digital revolution, who shot The Adventures of Tintin in performance capture in 2011) is not a technophobe. He's deliberate.</p>
<p>The more likely reality is that Spielberg has very specific standards for what enters his creative process. And AI, at least as it exists today, doesn't meet them for what he needs at his level. That's not the same as AI being wrong for film. It's a craftsman with extremely high tolerance thresholds saying the tool isn't ready for his particular use case.</p>
<p>That's a reasonable position. It's also a position of privilege that only a filmmaker of his standing can afford.</p>

<h2>The No AI Film Festival Problem</h2>
<p>The no AI festival is more interesting to unpack.</p>
<p>On one level, I understand it. There is legitimate concern about what happens when AI-generated content floods distribution channels, when faces and voices are synthesized without consent, when the line between real and fabricated disappears. Those are serious issues that go beyond aesthetics.</p>
<p>But the festival framing (films made without AI as a badge of purity) sets up a false dichotomy that I find frustrating from a production standpoint.</p>
<p>Think of an ordinary commercial from ten years ago. It ran on digital color grading, CGI product shots, digital sound design, and a non-linear editing system. None of those are "natural." They are all tools that changed how film was made when they arrived, were resisted by parts of the industry, and are now completely unremarkable.</p>
<p>The question was never "is the tool natural?" It was always "does the work have integrity?"</p>

<h2>Where I Stand</h2>
<p>I use AI in my work. I use it for pre-visualization, for concept iteration, for reducing the cost of testing ideas before committing to production. I do not use it to replace the parts of filmmaking that require human presence, judgment, and taste.</p>
<p>That line is not arbitrary for me. It comes from years on set, understanding which parts of a production can be systematized and which cannot.</p>
<p>The camera day is not the expensive part of production. The expensive part is all the uncertainty that precedes it: bad briefs, misaligned expectations, late-stage creative pivots, direction that gets lost between concept and execution. AI helps compress those costs significantly. The creative direction itself (knowing what story to tell, how to tell it, what to cut) remains human work.</p>

<h2>The Real Divide Isn't AI vs No AI</h2>
<p>What the Spielberg moment and the No AI Festival both reveal is a real underlying tension: who benefits when production gets cheaper?</p>
<p>If AI tools lower the cost of filmmaking, more people can make films. That sounds democratizing. But it also means the market gets flooded, distribution becomes harder to navigate, and the craft signals that used to separate good work from average work become harder to read.</p>
<p>Spielberg doesn't need to worry about that. His name is a distribution mechanism on its own.</p>
<p>Independent filmmakers and mid-career directors do need to worry about it. And the answer isn't to refuse the tools. It's to develop such a specific and recognizable creative voice that the tools become irrelevant to whether your work stands out.</p>
<p>The no AI position is ultimately a statement about identity, not craft. The real craft question is what you do with the tools, not which ones you refuse.</p>

<h2>The Practical Next Step</h2>
<p>If you are a filmmaker watching this debate, here is what I would focus on: build a clear creative point of view, develop your editorial instincts, and use every tool, AI or otherwise, in service of that. When your specific perspective is strong enough, the conversation about which tools you used becomes secondary.</p>
<p>Spielberg's "yet" is telling. The conversation is not over. It is just beginning.</p>
