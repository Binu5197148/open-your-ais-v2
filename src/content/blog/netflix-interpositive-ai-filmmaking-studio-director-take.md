<h2>The headline was big. The product is narrow.</h2>

<p>On March 5, 2026, Netflix announced it had acquired InterPositive, the AI filmmaking company Ben Affleck founded. Terms were not disclosed. If you work in production, your feed had two reactions that week: panic or hype.</p>

<p>I think both are lazy.</p>

<p>Netflix did not buy a machine that writes films from a sentence. It bought a way to get more out of footage a crew already shot. And if you run a small company, the most useful line in the whole announcement is the one almost nobody quoted: you cannot buy it.</p>

<h2>What does Ben Affleck's AI company do?</h2>

<p>InterPositive builds a small model for one production at a time. The model is trained on that production's own dailies and then used in post-production. TheWrap describes a "mini-model" optimized for that one film or show, specialized in post-production fixes like adding missing shots, correcting lighting or enhancing backgrounds. The same report carries Netflix's own framing: this is not text prompts, it is tooling that fits into real production workflows, and it is being integrated with Eyeline, Netflix's in-house VFX and research unit.</p>

<p>According to heise, the whole team of sixteen moved to Netflix and Affleck stays on as a senior advisor. Netflix's Elizabeth Stone also said it is not yet a complete, ready-to-go tool for every production.</p>

<p>So the honest summary is three lines. It works after the shoot, not instead of it. It learns from what the camera recorded. And it is unfinished.</p>

<h2>Why can't you buy the Netflix AI tool?</h2>

<p>Because Netflix said so. The company stated it will make the technology available to its creative partners and does not plan to sell it commercially. There is no price list, no waitlist and no public release date that we could find as of November 16, 2026.</p>

<p>That changes what the deal means for everyone outside the Netflix system. For a production already working with Netflix, it may become one more service inside the post pipeline. For an independent producer in São Paulo or anywhere else, it is a signal about direction, not a product to plan around.</p>

<p>Signals are still worth reading. This one says a platform with every option on the table chose a tool that depends on good dailies. It did not choose a prompt box.</p>

<h2>Where most people get it wrong</h2>

<p>The common post is, "AI is replacing filmmaking." No. In this deal, AI is aimed at the expensive gap between what was shot and what the cut needs.</p>

<p>Filmmaking still needs taste, emotional timing, casting instincts, rhythm, and narrative judgment. No model gives you that. It gives you options. A lot of options. Sometimes too many.</p>

<p>The bottleneck is no longer execution. The bottleneck is direction.</p>

<p>That is why weak creators will feel threatened and strong creators will compound. If your value is only pushing buttons in post, yes, this is dangerous. If your value is vision and decision quality, this is leverage.</p>

<h2>The production math, corrected</h2>

<p>The first version of this article put the savings in pre-production. The reporting puts them at the other end:</p>

<ul><li><strong>The missing shot gets cheaper:</strong> an insert nobody captured can be extended from real footage instead of costing a pickup day</li><li><strong>Fixes move earlier in post:</strong> relighting and background repair start from a model that already knows the look of the film</li><li><strong>Coverage matters more, not less:</strong> the model is only as good as the dailies it learned from</li><li><strong>Approval becomes the job:</strong> fewer people doing repetitive repair by hand, more people deciding which generated frame is honest to the scene</li></ul>

<p>That is not the death of cinema. It is pressure on the repetitive part of finishing.</p>

<h2>What I would do if I ran a small studio today</h2>

<p>If you run a small production company, do not copy Netflix. You cannot, and you do not need to. Learn from the logic.</p>

<p>Netflix trains on its own footage and keeps the model inside the house. The small studio version of that idea is not a custom model. It is discipline about your own material: references you own, decisions written down, and one person accountable for what ships. The generative tools you can rent today cover the early stages, which is a different job from the one InterPositive does, and they fit the same rule. AI for speed, humans for meaning.</p>

<p>Copy this sheet into the production bible and fill it in before the first generation:</p>

<pre><code>SMALL STUDIO HYBRID PIPELINE (one page)

Project:                      Client:
Accountable lead (one name):

STAGE              TOOL DOES                  HUMAN DECIDES
Treatment          variations of the idea     which idea is true to the brand
Look and boards    mood tests, rough frames   the one look, signed by the lead
Client previs      near-final concept         what the client approves, in writing
Shoot              nothing                    casting, performance, the take to print
Post               cleanup, extensions        every synthetic frame, logged per shot
Delivery           versions and formats       final check against the approved look

RULES
[ ] Every reference is ours or licensed. Source noted.
[ ] No model trains on client footage without it in the contract.
[ ] Synthetic shots are listed in the delivery notes.
[ ] If nobody can say why a frame is in the cut, it comes out.
</code></pre>

<p>Studios that ignore this will become slow and expensive. Studios that automate everything will become generic and forgettable. The winners are in the middle, but they move fast.</p>

<h2>My hard opinion</h2>

<p>Netflix did not buy a way to skip the shoot. It bought a way to get more out of the shoot it already paid for.</p>

<p>If you are a filmmaker, this is your wake-up call. Stop debating whether AI belongs in production. It is already in production.</p>

<p>The question that matters is simple: are you steering it, or being displaced by someone who does?</p>

<p><strong>Bottom line:</strong> AI will not replace directors. It will expose who was never directing in the first place.</p>

<h2>What this looks like for independent creators (not studios)</h2>
<p>The Netflix story gets a lot of attention because Netflix is Netflix. The implication for an independent creator running a one-person operation in São Paulo or anywhere outside the studio system is different and worth saying separately.</p>
<p>The independent creator never had the bottleneck a studio is trying to solve. You were not running a long development cycle with three teams of executives. You were already moving fast. What this acquisition signals is that the studios are building their own AI tooling in-house instead of waiting for a vendor.</p>
<p>The advantage shifts. A studio can now fix in post what used to need a pickup day, with a tool you will not get. What you have is the rented tools everyone has, and a shorter line between the idea and the person who decides. Both sides converge on speed. The differentiator becomes something else.</p>

<h2>The differentiator that survives</h2>
<p>What survives this convergence is point of view. A studio can buy the InterPositive stack. They cannot buy a specific filmmaker's worldview, the specific lens through which a story gets told, the specific cultural and personal experience that shapes which decisions feel right. That is the asset an independent creator owns and Netflix cannot acquire.</p>
<p>If you are sharpening anything in 2026, sharpen that. The tools are commodities. The lens is not.</p>
