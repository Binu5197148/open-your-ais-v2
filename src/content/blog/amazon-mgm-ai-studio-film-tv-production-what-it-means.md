<p><em>Updated November 13, 2026. This article was written in March, when Amazon MGM's AI tools were a closed beta with results promised for May. The state today is different. On May 27 Amazon MGM Studios and AWS announced Project Nara, their AI production platform, together with the GenAI Creators' Fund and three AI animated series ordered for Prime Video. Two days later one of the three creators walked out. Nara is still not something you can sign up for. This is a reading of public announcements and trade coverage, not a hands-on test, because nobody outside the program has access. I found no newer announcement about Nara as of this date.</em></p>

<h2>What Amazon said in February</h2>
<p>In February 2026, <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/04/amazon-to-begin-testing-ai-tools-for-film-and-tv-production-next-month/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">TechCrunch reported</a>, following Reuters, that Amazon MGM Studios would begin a closed beta of its AI production tools in March, with results expected to be shared by May. The studio had set up a dedicated internal AI Studio the year before, led by Albert Cheng, a longtime Amazon entertainment executive.</p>
<p>The tools focused on character consistency across shots, script analysis, shot composition and visual effects support. The named collaborators were Robert Stromberg (director of Maleficent), Kunal Nayyar and former Pixar animator Colin Brady.</p>
<p>This was not a startup experiment. It was one of the largest studios in the world systematizing AI at the production level.</p>

<h2>What is Project Nara, and can you use Amazon's AI studio?</h2>
<p>Short answer: Project Nara is the platform that came out of that work, and no, you cannot use it unless Amazon picks you.</p>
<p>On May 27, 2026, at the AI on the Lot conference at Culver Studios, Amazon MGM Studios and AWS announced three things at once, <a href="https://www.tvtechnology.com/production/amazon-mgm-studios-aws-launch-the-genai-creators-fund" target="_blank" rel="noopener">as TV Technology reported that day</a>.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Project Nara.</strong> A collaborative production workspace built on AWS, for animation and live action. It connects AI production agents to tools crews already use: Maya, Blender, Nuke, Unreal Engine and the Adobe suite. It routes each task to whichever model fits it, mixing third-party video models with models trained on Amazon MGM's own library. It also records provenance, meaning where each piece of content came from.</li>
  <li><strong>The GenAI Creators' Fund.</strong> Grants for proofs of concept and shorts, aimed at digital creators with large audiences, professional filmmakers building AI workflows, and startups. Amazon has not disclosed the size of the grants.</li>
  <li><strong>Three AI animated series for Prime Video.</strong> Cupcake &amp; Friends from BuzzFeed Studios, Love, Diana Music Hunters from Albie Hecht of pocket.watch, and Punky Duck from Jorge R. Gutierrez. No release date was given.</li>
</ul>
<p>The access rule is the line that matters for everyone else. Nara is used exclusively by Amazon MGM Studios and by creators selected for the fund. The coverage I read gives no public application page, no price and no waitlist.</p>
<p><a href="https://the-decoder.com/amazon-builds-its-own-ai-production-platform-and-greenlights-three-ai-animated-series-for-prime-video/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Decoder added two details</a> on May 28. Each team had five weeks to produce its pilot. And Albert Cheng told Variety that all three projects involve human actors and voice talent.</p>

<h2>What happened to the closed beta results?</h2>
<p>I looked for the findings Amazon said it would share by May and did not find a published report. What arrived in May was a product launch and a slate, not a results document. That is worth saying plainly, because the February coverage set an expectation of evidence. What the public got is an announcement.</p>
<p>The closest thing to a finding is one sentence from Cheng, quoted by The Decoder: "One of the biggest complaints we hear from creators is: AI will not do what you want it to do." That is the same complaint every director working with these models has. Amazon's answer is to wrap the models in a pipeline with agents, existing software and its own trained models. Whether that fixes the complaint is something only the people inside the program can say today.</p>

<h2>The first creator left in two days</h2>
<p>On May 29, two days after the announcement, Jorge R. Gutierrez posted that he had decided to drop out of the AI program at Amazon and would not be making a Punky Duck series. <a href="https://80.lv/articles/jorge-r-gutierrez-drops-out-of-amazon-s-genai-creators-fund-program" target="_blank" rel="noopener">80 Level</a> and <a href="https://www.awn.com/news/jorge-gutierrez-pulls-out-prime-video-project-nara-ai-platform-series" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Animation World Network</a> both covered it, and both tie the decision to the reaction from the animation community.</p>
<p>Read that as production information, not gossip. A five-week pilot schedule is a cost argument. The people who normally fill the months that schedule removes are animators, and they answered within forty-eight hours. Any producer planning an AI animated series now has a public example of what the announcement alone can cost in talent and goodwill, before a single frame is released.</p>

<h2>What the tools were built to fix</h2>
<p>The capabilities Amazon described in February map to the expensive friction points in professional production, and Nara's feature list targets the same three.</p>
<p><strong>Character consistency across shots.</strong> This has been the most persistent failure in AI video. A character who looks different from shot to shot is unusable on a professional project. Training models on a studio's own library is one way to attack it, and it is a way only a library owner has.</p>
<p><strong>Script analysis.</strong> Breaking down a script to identify locations, cast requirements, VFX needs and scheduling dependencies happens before a single frame is shot. It is pattern recognition work, and machines handle it well.</p>
<p><strong>Shot composition assistance.</strong> Composition is where direction lives. But there is a difference between generating a composition and evaluating one. A tool that flags an eyeline problem or a continuity break is useful without taking the decision away from the director.</p>

<h2>What this means if you run a smaller operation</h2>
<p>When the largest studios build and own their AI production pipeline, they gain a cost advantage an independent producer cannot copy. Amazon spreads the development cost across hundreds of productions and trains on a library nobody else can license. A small production company depends on what is sold commercially.</p>
<p>The useful part is that Nara's architecture is not exotic. A pipeline that sends each task to the model that does it best is something a small team can assemble today with commercial tools. The models are the same family: video generators such as Kling and Seedance, reached through a hub, steered by references and a shot list. What a small team does not have is the trained-on-our-own-library layer and the provenance record. The first you cannot buy. The second you can start keeping by hand this week.</p>
<p>One correction to the March version of this page: it listed Sora 2 among the tools to learn. <a href="/blog/sora-2-api-open-what-producers-do-now/">OpenAI removed the Sora 2 API on September 24</a>, which is its own lesson about building on a platform you rent.</p>

<h2>A checklist to copy before you pitch an AI animated series</h2>
<p>Amazon's launch and the two days after it give you the questions a client or a platform will ask. Paste this into your project notes and answer each line in writing.</p>
<pre><code>AI SERIES PITCH CHECK
1. Which shots are generated, which are drawn or shot, and who decided?
2. Who are the human actors and voice talent, and are they credited?
3. Which models touch the project, and what does each license allow?
4. Where is the provenance record for every generated asset?
5. What happens to the schedule if one model is withdrawn mid-production?
6. Has the crew been told how AI is used, before the press release?
7. Who signs off each episode as finished, by name?</code></pre>
<p>Line 6 is the one the Punky Duck episode makes urgent. Line 5 is the one Sora made urgent.</p>

<h2>The bigger signal</h2>
<p><a href="/blog/netflix-interpositive-ai-filmmaking-studio-director-take/">Netflix bought InterPositive</a>. Amazon built Nara and ordered a slate on it. Google merged its creative tools into Flow. These are different expressions of the same bet: that AI-assisted production is a change in how professional content gets made, not a side experiment.</p>
<p>The studios are building these pipelines because they expect the cost and speed advantages to change who can afford to make what. How you respond is a strategic question, not a technical one. And the first public result of Amazon's version was not a pilot. It was a creator leaving.</p>
