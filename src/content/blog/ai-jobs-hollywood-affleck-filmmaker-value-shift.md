<h2>What Affleck Said, and Why It Matters</h2>
<p>In January 2026, on The Joe Rogan Experience, Ben Affleck said he doubted AI would be able to "write anything meaningful," and described its future role as "a tool just like sort of visual effects." He was also direct about the appeal: "It'll save us a lot of money, a lot of time." All three lines come from <a href="https://www.foxnews.com/media/ben-affleck-downplays-ai-fears-film-industry-says-laws-already-exist-protect-artists-likeness" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Fox News coverage of the interview</a>.</p>
<p>On March 5, Netflix announced it was acquiring InterPositive, the AI company Affleck had founded, bringing in the whole team and making him a senior advisor (<a href="http://about.netflix.com/en/news/why-interpositive-is-joining-netflix" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Netflix</a>). So the man calling AI a tool had been building one.</p>
<p>That is not a contradiction. It is the clearest statement of his position. And the reason it matters isn't that he's a celebrity. It's that he's someone who has operated at every level of the film industry: writer, actor, director, and producer. He's seen the inside of studio decisions, independent productions, and the political machinery that determines which projects get made and why. His perspective on value isn't abstract.</p>

<h2>What Does InterPositive Do?</h2>
<p>Per <a href="http://about.netflix.com/en/news/why-interpositive-is-joining-netflix" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Netflix</a>, the first model was trained on a proprietary dataset shot on a controlled soundstage, and it handles production problems such as missing shots, background swaps and wrong lighting. The tools use the terms cinematographers and directors already use, and the models focus on filmmaking technique rather than performance. Netflix's line is that the goal is to "empower storytellers, not replace them."</p>
<p>Three limits are worth keeping in view. Netflix says the technology goes only to in-house creative partners, not rival production companies, and <a href="https://restofworld.org/2026/netflix-interpositive-vfx-ai-automation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rest of World</a> reports that it remains unclear whether outside VFX vendors working on Netflix originals count. The price was undisclosed by Netflix, with reports of up to $600 million tied to performance targets (<a href="https://au.variety.com/2026/digital/news/netflix-600-million-ben-affleck-ai-film-startup-interpositive-34130" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Variety</a>). And I found no public case naming a title that used it, so what the tool does on a real production is still unproven in public.</p>

<h2>Which Film Jobs Are Safe?</h2>
<p>No job is safe by decree, and no one has published real numbers yet. The best available signals point in one direction, though. <a href="https://restofworld.org/2026/netflix-interpositive-vfx-ai-automation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rest of World</a> describes the exposed work as color grading, relighting and continuity fixes done frame by frame, and quotes a DNEG compositing supervisor saying the biggest impact will be at the entry level. <a href="https://deadline.com/2026/04/netflix-ben-affleck-ai-firm-interpositive-film-production-savings-1236770381/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Deadline</a> reported on an InterPositive filing that projected savings of at least 10% to 20% on below the line production. That is the company's own projection, not a measured result.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Most exposed:</strong> repeatable fixes on footage that already exists. Rotoscoping, cleanup, relighting a shot, matching continuity.</li>
<li><strong>Under pressure:</strong> junior post roles, which is also where people have always learned the craft. That is the part nobody has an answer for.</li>
<li><strong>Least exposed:</strong> decisions made in the room. What the scene is about, which take is true, how to direct an actor, what to tell a client.</li>
</ul>
<pre><code>Your own exposure check, in ten minutes

List what you billed for in the last three jobs. For each item:
1. Could someone specify it fully in one sentence?
2. Is the right answer the same for every project?
3. Does it happen after the footage exists?
4. Would a client notice who did it?

Three yes and a no on question 4: that item is exposed.
Move your pitch toward the items that fail this test.</code></pre>

<h2>The Value Shift, Explained</h2>
<p>This is what "value shift" means in practical terms for working filmmakers:</p>
<p><strong>What is losing value:</strong> Technical execution of defined tasks. If you know how to operate a camera but don't know <em>why</em> you're framing a shot, AI will price you out. If you can edit footage mechanically but can't read the emotional arc of a scene, that skill is being automated. Technical proficiency without creative judgment is losing its market premium.</p>
<p><strong>What is gaining value:</strong> The ability to decide what to make and why. Creative direction, not just in the formal title sense, but in the fundamental sense of having taste, knowing what an audience needs to feel, and making hundreds of small decisions that add up to a coherent piece of work. Client communication, narrative strategy, brand alignment. On-set leadership and talent direction. The relationship-building that converts projects into ongoing partnerships.</p>
<p>None of those are AI-automatable, because they require context that only humans have: the history of a client relationship, the emotional subtext of a scene, the cultural specificity of a market, the intuition built from years of watching what works and what doesn't.</p>

<h2>Hollywood Is Already Running This Experiment</h2>
<p>The studios aren't waiting for the theoretical value shift to happen. Netflix bought a company for this. It also opened Eyeline Studios in Hyderabad in March, a facility built for generative visual effects, and Los Angeles County lost 41,000 film and TV jobs over three years, both per <a href="https://restofworld.org/2026/netflix-interpositive-vfx-ai-automation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Rest of World</a>. That job figure has several causes, and AI is not shown to be the main one. Still, a shrinking market is the one these tools are arriving in.</p>
<p>The useful posture is to treat AI as a production upgrade rather than a threat, and to spend the time it saves on more projects or on going deeper into the ones you have.</p>

<h2>The Real Risk: Competing on Price Instead of Value</h2>
<p>The dangerous response to AI pressure in Hollywood isn't fear of automation. It's the temptation to compete on price. When AI makes "good enough" content cheaper to produce, some filmmakers will try to undercut AI costs by offering their human work at bargain rates. That's a race they will lose. AI will always be cheaper at producing "good enough."</p>
<p>The right response is to compete on value: to specialize in the decisions, relationships, and creative judgment that make work exceptional rather than acceptable. That means being clear about what you offer that AI doesn't. It means saying no to projects where you'd be competing with AI on price and seeking out projects where human judgment is the product.</p>

<h2>What to Change Based on This Reality</h2>
<p>The move for anyone selling production is to shift the offer:</p>
<ul>
<li>From: "I can produce X quantity of content in Y timeline"</li>
<li>To: "I can build a creative strategy that serves your brand's specific goals and execute it at a level that AI can't match"</li>
</ul>
<p>That shift is harder to sell than a production quote. It requires more client education. But it's sustainable in a way that competing with AI on volume and price is not. The filmmakers who acknowledge it early and adapt their positioning accordingly are going to be the ones still standing when the dust settles.</p>

<h2>A better conversation with new clients</h2>
<p>When a client asks what makes your work different from a cheaper AI-assisted shop, don't list features. Ask what their last campaign did for the business. If they can't answer with specifics, that is the opening.</p>
<p>The decision they face is not "human or AI" but "vendor I trust to think about my problem or vendor who delivers files." A vendor who delivers files is competing on price, and AI will eventually win that fight. A vendor who thinks about the problem is selling something AI cannot replicate, regardless of what tools the vendor uses internally.</p>
<p>The shift has started in commercial work, and the InterPositive deal shows it has reached studio production too, even if the public evidence of use is still thin.</p>
