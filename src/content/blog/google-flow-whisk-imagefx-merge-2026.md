<h2>Correction and update</h2>
<p>Updated Oct 12, 2026: the March version of this article said Flow had no connection to Veo and asked Google to merge them. That was wrong. Flow launched in May 2025 as Google's AI filmmaking tool built for Veo, and the February 2026 update moved Whisk and ImageFX into it, so images and video already lived in one place. This version corrects that, replaces feature claims we could not verify with the ones Google documents, removes first-person usage claims and comparisons that were not backed by a test, and adds how the free tier works today. It is a documentary analysis of Google's own pages, not a hands-on review.</p>
</section>

<h2>The End of Tool Hopping</h2>
<p>On February 25, 2026, Google announced that the best parts of its two image experiments, Whisk and ImageFX, were moving into Flow. Starting in March, people could opt in to transfer their Whisk and ImageFX projects and assets into their Flow library.</p>
<p>If you used AI image tools over the past year, you know the pain this addresses. You would create something in one tool, realize you needed a feature from another, export, import, lose quality, adjust settings, export again. It is the AI equivalent of tape-to-tape editing: technically functional, creatively soul-crushing.</p>
<p>The merge matters less as a product announcement than as a direction. Generation, editing and animation in one workspace means fewer places where a decision gets lost in transit. Every handoff is where quality dies.</p>

<h2>What Whisk and ImageFX Were</h2>
<p><strong>ImageFX</strong> was Google's text-to-image generator built on the Imagen models. You typed a prompt, you got an image. If it was not right, you typed another prompt.</p>
<p><strong>Whisk</strong> was the more experimental tool, built around references: one image for the subject, one for the scene, one for the style, and Whisk combined them. Think of it as "what if this person was in this place, drawn in this style?"</p>
<p><strong>Flow</strong> is older than both merges suggest. Google introduced it at I/O on May 20, 2025 as an AI filmmaking tool designed for Veo, with camera controls, a Scenebuilder for extending shots, and asset management. It evolved from VideoFX. So the February update did not connect images to video. It pulled the image tools into the place where the video already was.</p>

<h2>What Flow Delivers Now</h2>
<p>Google's February update lists these, and they are the ones worth knowing before you open it:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nano Banana built in.</strong> Image generation sits in the core of Flow, and the images you make can be used as ingredients and frames for Veo video generations without leaving the app.</li>
<li><strong>Free image generation.</strong> Google states that you can generate images for free in Flow.</li>
<li><strong>Lasso editing.</strong> Select an area of an image and describe the change in plain language, such as removing a person or adding an element.</li>
<li><strong>Drawing on the image.</strong> Mark directly on the frame to show what should change.</li>
<li><strong>Clip extension.</strong> Extend a clip and let Flow generate what happens next.</li>
<li><strong>Object editing in video.</strong> Add or remove objects in a clip with a text prompt.</li>
<li><strong>Camera motion.</strong> Direct pans and zooms instead of hoping the prompt implies them.</li>
</ul>
<p>Read that list as a director and the interesting item is not any single feature. It is that the frame you approve and the shot you animate are now the same asset. That is the handoff that used to cost the most.</p>

<h2>How do you use Google Flow for free?</h2>
<p>Short answer: every Google account gets a daily allowance, and it is enough to learn the tool, not to run a job on it. According to the <a href="https://support.google.com/flow/answer/16526234?hl=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Google Flow Help Center</a>:</p>
<ul>
<li>With or without a subscription, you receive 50 Flow credits per day.</li>
<li>Unused daily credits do not roll over, and the daily refresh is triggered by your first generation.</li>
<li>Google AI Plus adds 200 credits a month and Google AI Pro adds 1,000. Monthly credits do not roll over either.</li>
<li>Credit cost depends on the model you pick, so 50 credits do not always buy the same number of clips.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Getting started: a first image-to-video pass</h2>
<p>A sequence a beginner can run on the free allowance, built only on the features Google documents:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Open Flow</strong> at <a href="https://labs.google/fx/tools/flow" target="_blank" rel="noopener">labs.google/fx/tools/flow</a> with a Google account. You must be 18 or older.</li>
<li><strong>Write the frame before the shot.</strong> Generate the still first. Images are the cheapest place to make decisions, and Google says image generation in Flow is free.</li>
<li><strong>Fix the frame, not the prompt.</strong> When one area is wrong, use the lasso and describe only that change instead of regenerating the whole image.</li>
<li><strong>Save it as an ingredient.</strong> Keep the approved still in your library so the next generations start from the same face, product or set.</li>
<li><strong>Animate last.</strong> Use the approved image as the frame for a Veo generation, and give the camera an instruction: a slow push, a pan, a zoom.</li>
<li><strong>Extend only what works.</strong> If the first seconds hold, extend the clip rather than starting over.</li>
<li><strong>Check your balance</strong> under your profile picture before a long session, so the credits do not run out in the middle of a sequence.</li>
</ol>
<p>The order is the method: decide in stills, spend on motion only after the frame is approved. It is the same rule that keeps a real shoot on budget, and the same reason <a href="/blog/ai-storytelling-video-story-first-method-director/">story comes before generation</a> in any AI piece worth watching.</p>

<h2>Where Flow fits for a director</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pitch and mood boards:</strong> generating a coherent set of frames for a commercial concept, organised as assets in one project.</li>
<li><strong>Previs:</strong> turning approved frames into rough motion to show a client how a shot moves before anyone books a crew.</li>
<li><strong>Social content:</strong> quick assets where the tool's speed matters more than a signature look.</li>
</ul>
<p>Where <a href="/blog/midjourney-v7-commercial-concept-boards-visual-development-directors-guide/">Midjourney still makes more sense</a>: anything where the look itself is the product. Hero visuals and art-led work reward a model with a strong aesthetic signature, and that is a different job from organising a sequence.</p>

<h2>What Still Needs Work</h2>
<h3>The free tier is a classroom, not a studio</h3>
<p>Fifty credits a day that do not roll over are enough to learn the order of operations. They are not enough to iterate a commercial sequence, where the useful generation is often the fifteenth.</p>
<h3>Migration is opt-in</h3>
<p>Google's own wording is that you can opt in to transfer your Whisk and ImageFX projects. Treat that as a prompt to download anything important yourself before relying on the transfer.</p>
<h3>Automation lives elsewhere</h3>
<p>Flow is an app. If you are building an automated content pipeline, the models are reached through Google's developer APIs, not through Flow's interface, so plan that work separately.</p>
<h3>Prompt sensitivity</h3>
<p>Small changes in phrasing can still produce very different results, which is exactly why the method above fixes decisions in stills before spending on motion.</p>

<h2>The Big Picture</h2>
<p>Flow shows how Google now thinks about creative tools: not separate experiments hoping to connect, but one workspace where image, edit and motion share assets. That is what professional adoption needs. Nobody wants five interfaces, five subscriptions and five places to lose a file.</p>
<p>The March version of this piece ended by asking Google to connect images and video. The honest update is that the connection already existed, and the real question for a working director is narrower: whether the credit model fits the number of iterations a client job needs. For learning and for previs, the free allowance is a fair way to find out.</p>
