<p><em>Updated September 28, 2026. This is an analysis for producers, not legal advice and not a hands-on test. Three things moved in September. Suno's new terms took effect on September 3 and changed how downloads and commercial use work. Suno v6 launched on September 9 with Warner Music as a licensing partner. <p><em>Updated August 31, 2026. This was written on March 15, 2026, and describes the rights picture as it stood then. Terms of service on AI music platforms change without announcement, and the tier you pay for is the one that decides what you own, so confirm the current wording on your own account before you deliver a track to broadcast or sync. What has not changed is the question this piece exists to ask, which is who is actually liable when a client legal team looks at the invoice.</em></p>

<h2>The Music Nobody Asked About Until the Invoice Arrived</h2>
<p>Every producer I know has used AI-generated music at some point in the past two years. For scratch tracks, presentation reels, internal content, social posts: AI music fills gaps that previously required either a music budget or royalty-free library tracks that sounded like they were composed in 2009. Suno in particular has become the default for this use case: open browser, describe the vibe, download, cut in. Fast, free on the basic plan, and good enough for most purposes.</p>
<p>The question nobody asks until a client's legal team does: what are the actual commercial rights you hold to that track, and what happens when it goes to broadcast, sync, or a platform that checks?</p>
<p>The answer in 2026 is more specific than it was two years ago, and some of the specifics are not what people assume.</p>

<h2>What Suno's Current License Actually Says</h2>
<p>Three different questions get mixed up here, and the terms answer each one separately.</p>
<p><strong>Who holds Suno's rights in the song.</strong> On Pro and Premier, the <a href="https://suno.com/terms-of-service" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Terms of Service effective September 3, 2026</a> say Suno "hereby assigns to you all of its right, title and interest" in output it owns that you generated through the service. Suno's own help center puts it plainly: songs made on a Pro or Premier plan are yours, and on the free Basic plan Suno keeps ownership and you may only use them for non-commercial purposes.</p>
<p><strong>Whether there is any copyright to hold.</strong> This is the part people miss. The same terms say Suno "makes no representation or warranty to you that any copyright will vest in any Output." Suno can only hand over what it has. Its <a href="https://help.suno.com/en/articles/2746945" target="_blank" rel="noopener">help article on copyright</a> notes that under US law music made entirely by AI would not qualify for copyright protection, while lyrics you wrote yourself are yours. So you may own the song and still have little to enforce against someone who makes something close to it.</p>
<p><strong>Whether you can use it commercially.</strong> Since September 3 that depends on a permitted download from a paid plan, covered in the September section below.</p>
<p>For work where functional music is needed and exclusivity is not the requirement, a paid plan is workable for many use cases. For work where a unique, defensible sound is part of the brief, the missing copyright guarantee is the problem, not the assignment.</p>

<h2>The Free Plan Limitation Nobody Notices Until Too Late</h2>
<p>Tracks generated on Suno's free plan are restricted to non-commercial use only. They cannot be monetized, licensed, or used in content that earns revenue, including YouTube videos that have AdSense enabled. This restriction persists even if you later subscribe. The generation context at the time of creation is what determines the license, not your current subscription status.</p>
<p>In production workflows where multiple team members use the tool, this creates a tracking problem. If an editor generated a scratch track on a free account and it made it to final delivery, the commercial license is not there regardless of what the production company's subscription level is. This is the most common compliance failure I've seen in the context of AI music in production: not intentional misuse, but a free-tier track that traveled from scratch to final cut without anyone checking the generation account's license tier.</p>
<p>The operational fix is simple: designate a single production account on a paid tier for all AI music generation, and never import tracks generated on free accounts into a client project timeline, regardless of how good the scratch track sounds.</p>

<h2>The Warner Music Deal and the Lawsuit Running in Parallel</h2>
<p>In late 2025, Suno announced a partnership with Warner Music Group that is structured to influence how licensed AI music models are developed. The deal signals that at least one major label sees a path to working with AI music platforms rather than only litigating against them. The details of what the partnership covers (training data licensing, revenue sharing, attribution requirements) are not fully public.</p>
<p>What is fully public: Suno is still subject to an ongoing lawsuit from other major record labels that is not covered by the Warner deal. The legal theory in that case centers on training data copyright: whether the models were trained on copyrighted recordings without authorization. The case will set precedent that affects every AI music platform, not just Suno.</p>
<p>For commercial producers, the lawsuit running in parallel with the Warner partnership creates an ambiguous risk profile. The Warner relationship suggests that licensed AI music is achievable and that Suno is moving toward it. The ongoing litigation suggests that the current situation is not fully resolved from a rights clearance perspective.</p>

<h2>Platform Disclosure Requirements Are Now Enforced</h2>
<p>Starting in late 2025, Spotify and Apple Music began enforcing the DDEX industry standard for AI disclosure. Tracks using Suno-generated audio must be flagged during upload through distributors. This is not optional. Distributors who submit AI-generated content without the proper DDEX flag can face platform violations. The flagging requirement exists at the distributor level, not just at the user level.</p>
<p>For video sync (placing a track into a film, advertisement, or online video), the disclosure requirements are handled differently per platform and broadcaster. For broadcast delivery in the UK and many EU markets, the specific rights status of AI-generated music must be documented in cue sheets. The ambiguity in Suno's training data rights status makes this documentation difficult to complete in a way that satisfies professional clearance requirements.</p>

<h2>What changed in September 2026</h2>
<p><strong>Downloads now decide commercial use.</strong> Under the terms effective September 3, 2026, songs downloaded on a paid plan can be used commercially. Pro includes 20 downloads a month and Premier 60. Free accounts get up to seven lifetime trial downloads, and Suno says those trial downloads are not eligible for commercial use. The limits apply to every download from September 3 on, including songs made before that date. In practice, the download is now the moment the commercial right attaches, so the account that clicks download matters as much as the account that generated the song.</p>
<p><strong>The Warner deal became a model.</strong> Warner settled with Suno in November 2025 and left the 2024 lawsuit. Suno v6, released September 9, 2026, lists Warner among its licensing partners, and Suno says v6 was trained on licensed content from its partners plus creations and preference signals from users.</p>
<p><strong>The lawsuit did not go away. It doubled.</strong> On September 18, Sony Music and Universal Music filed a new complaint in federal court in Massachusetts naming 60,202 recordings and seeking up to $150,000 per work, a ceiling above $9 billion. Their argument is that training a new model on the outputs of the old one does not clean the old one. Suno calls the claims "fundamentally flawed on both the facts and the law." The original 2024 case, which covers 560 works, is still running in parallel. The March prediction at the end of this piece, that broadcast clearance was 12 to 24 months away, now looks optimistic.</p>

<h2>Can you make a Suno song copyright free?</h2>
<p>Not in the way a composer's work is, and this is the question people type most often. On a paid plan Suno assigns its rights in the song to you, so on paper the track is yours to use and sell. What nobody can give you is a guarantee that copyright exists in a fully generated track. Suno's terms say so directly. That has two consequences on a job. You may have little to enforce if someone else releases something close, and you cannot hand a client the same clean chain of rights a composer's work-for-hire contract would give them. Lyrics you wrote yourself are a different case, and can be protected as your writing.</p>
<p>The related searches, how to upload a cover or how to get past a copyright block on upload, are asking for the opposite of what a production needs. If a platform flags a track, removing the flag does not remove the problem. It moves the problem into your client's legal department.</p>
<p>The checklist I would run before a Suno track leaves the edit suite:</p>
<ol>
  <li>Generated and downloaded on the production's own paid account, not an editor's free one.</li>
  <li>Downloaded on or after the plan was active, with the download logged in the project folder.</li>
  <li>Use is social, pitch or internal, not broadcast, cinema or a sync that asks for a clearance letter.</li>
  <li>AI disclosure set correctly if the track goes to distribution.</li>
  <li>The client told in writing that the track is AI generated, that Suno's rights were assigned under a paid plan, and that copyright protection is not guaranteed.</li>
</ol>
<p>Read the source documents yourself before a delivery: <a href="https://suno.com/terms-of-service" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Suno Terms of Service</a>, <a href="https://help.suno.com/en/articles/2416769" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Suno's help page on ownership</a> and <a href="https://suno.com/blog/suno-updates-tos" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Suno's post on the September downloads policy</a>.</p>

<h2>The Practical Recommendation</h2>
<p>Use Suno for what it's genuinely good at without legal exposure: scratch tracks, pitches, internal presentations, and social content on platforms where you're not seeking sync licensing or distributing commercially through the music supply chain. For those use cases, a paid plan gives you Suno's rights in the track and commercial use through a permitted download, which is enough for most practical purposes.</p>
<p>For broadcast, cinema, commercial advertising with music licensing requirements, or any situation where the client or broadcaster will ask for a clearance letter or music rights documentation: use licensed music, whether that's library music with clear rights documentation or a work-for-hire composer who can provide the copyright ownership you need. AI music platforms are not there yet for that tier of commercial use, and the legal landscape won't be fully settled until the ongoing litigation concludes.</p>
<p>The timeline for when AI music reaches full commercial clearability at the broadcast level is probably 12-24 months, depending on how the major label lawsuits resolve. Until then, the tier distinction is clear: scratch and social, yes. Broadcast and sync, not yet.</p>
