<p><em>Updated November 4, 2026. This piece was written in March, while the talks were still open, and the negotiation it describes is over. SAG-AFTRA members ratified the 2026 TV/Theatrical agreement on June 4, 2026, with 91.42% voting yes on a turnout of 19.25%. It runs through June 30, 2030. The final deal did not create a flat royalty on AI performers. It created a test: a producer has to show that a synthetic performer brings "significant additional value" over a union actor and over that actor's digital replica. The section on the ratified terms is new, and so is the checklist at the end. The rest is the March reading, kept as a record. This is an analysis of public reporting, not legal advice.</em></p>

<h2>The Negotiation That Changes Commercial Production Math</h2>
<p>On March 6, 2026, SAG-AFTRA and the AMPTP announced a one-week extension of contract negotiations that had begun unusually early on February 9. The reason negotiations started early is significant: both sides understand that AI is the central issue in the 2026 contracts, and neither side is underestimating the complexity of what's being negotiated.</p>
<p>For commercial producers, the 2026 contract discussions matter directly. The productions most affected by AI performer technology are not feature films or television series. They are exactly the high-volume commercial and advertising productions that use AI-generated spokespeople, AI voice cloning, and AI-synthesized talent to manage costs at scale. If you're using HeyGen, ElevenLabs voice cloning, or any AI generation tool to create synthetic performances in commercial content, these negotiations determine the legal and financial structure of that use.</p>

<h2>The Tilly Tax: The Royalty Idea Discussed in March</h2>
<p>The central AI provision in SAG-AFTRA's 2026 negotiating position is a royalty fee, informally called the "Tilly tax" after an early discussion around it, that would require producers to pay SAG-AFTRA whenever AI-generated performers are used in place of real talent in productions covered by the agreement. The stated goal is to make AI performers financially comparable to hiring real people, removing the cost incentive for substituting generated performances for real ones.</p>
<p>The mechanism being proposed works like this: if you use an AI-generated actor, voice, or digital replica in a production that would otherwise be covered by SAG-AFTRA's jurisdiction, you pay a fee to the union comparable to what you would have paid in wages and residuals to a real member performing the same role. The union's position is that AI-generated performances should not create a cost arbitrage that incentivizes replacing union members with synthetic alternatives.</p>
<p>From a production standpoint, the Tilly tax would change the economics of AI spokesperson content in two directions: it would increase the cost of AI-generated performances in covered productions, and it would create clear financial accountability for which productions choose AI over real talent. For productions currently below SAG-AFTRA's jurisdiction threshold, the immediate impact may be limited, but the precedent being set will extend over time.</p>

<h2>What Did the 2026 SAG-AFTRA Contract Decide on AI?</h2>
<p>The royalty described above did not make it into the ratified text in that form. According to the reporting on the final deal by <a href="https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/labor-unions/sag-aftra-members-ratify-new-contract/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">TheWrap</a> and <a href="https://fortune.com/2026/06/05/sag-aftra-approves-four-year-contract-studios-streamers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Fortune</a>, the AI terms work like this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Humans first, in writing.</strong> Studios agreed to use union actors "overwhelmingly."</li>
<li><strong>A value test for synthetics.</strong> A synthetic performer, meaning an AI-generated actor who is not a replica of a real person, is allowed only if it brings "significant additional value." The comparison is against a union performer and also against a digital replica of one. TheWrap notes that the contract does not define that phrase.</li>
<li><strong>An arbitrator, not a fee schedule.</strong> If the union and the producer disagree, SAG-AFTRA can go to arbitration and seek damages that are not necessarily capped at what a human performer would have been paid.</li>
<li><strong>Replicas stay paid.</strong> A digital replica's performance is based on the voice and likeness of a member who is owed compensation, in the words of the union's chief negotiator quoted by TheWrap.</li>
<li><strong>No strike over this until 2030.</strong> The union gets notice and a chance to bargain if studios start using synthetic actors, but the four year term means the next leverage point is the 2030 renewal.</li>
</ul>
<p>The practical reading for a producer: the March fear was a price tag. What arrived is a burden of proof. You no longer ask "how much does the synthetic actor cost in union fees," you ask "can I defend in front of an arbitrator why this role needed a synthetic performer instead of a person." For most commercial work, that question has an uncomfortable answer, and that is the point of the clause.</p>
<p>One caveat on scope. This is the TV/Theatrical agreement. Commercials run on their own contract, described in the next section, and that contract is the one most brand productions sign.</p>

<h2>What the 2025 Commercials Contracts Already Changed</h2>
<p>Before the 2026 negotiations, SAG-AFTRA's 2025 Commercials Contracts already introduced the first contractual limitations on AI training data use from members' performances. In the approved commercial contract, advertisers and agencies must now obtain SAG-AFTRA permission before they can authorize any third parties to use commercial performance material to train AI systems.</p>
<p>For production companies, this means that any commercial production featuring SAG-AFTRA talent now includes a restriction on the downstream use of that talent's performance data for AI training purposes. If you want to train an AI voice clone or video avatar model on footage from a union production, you need explicit permission from SAG-AFTRA, and the talent involved has recourse if their performance is used without that permission.</p>
<p>This is already active. The Commercials Contracts cover the term that began on April 1, 2025, according to the <a href="https://www.dglaw.com/new-sag-aftra-commercials-contract-is-now-in-effect/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Davis+Gilbert summary</a>, after the union board approved the deal on April 27, 2025, per <a href="https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2025/04/2025-sag-aftra-commercials-contracts-moas-transform-in-the-digital-age" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DLA Piper</a>. It affects any commercial production that used SAG-AFTRA talent since then, which is the majority of professional commercial production in North America. If your production company uses commercial footage as AI training data, or if a vendor you work with does, the 2025 Commercials Contracts create legal exposure that needs to be addressed in your production agreements and vendor contracts.</p>

<p>The same contract also covers the two cases that matter most to AI production. A producer needs clear and conspicuous consent from a performer before creating or using that performer's digital replica, and there is a standard Digital Replica Rider for it. When a synthetic performer appears next to at least one human principal on camera, DLA Piper's summary describes a fixed payment of 1.5 session fees plus the related contributions. For a commercial made entirely with synthetic performers, the producer and the union negotiate in good faith over a contribution, if any. That clause is the closest thing to a "Tilly tax" that exists today, and it is a negotiation, not a rate card.</p>

<h2>Digital Twin Protections</h2>
<p>SAG-AFTRA's AI priorities also include explicit protection against the unauthorized creation of digital twins or clones of members' likenesses. This provision addresses tools that can generate a photorealistic replica of a real person's appearance and voice from limited source material, the category of tools that created the most immediate concern about AI in entertainment.</p>
<p>For commercial productions, this means that creating an AI version of a real person's likeness for use in advertising, without that person's explicit consent and appropriate compensation, is directly addressed in the union's negotiating position. The legal framework is being built around what was previously a gray area.</p>

<h2>What This Means for Commercial Producers Right Now</h2>
<p>Three practical implications for how you operate a commercial production company in 2026:</p>
<p>First, audit your current AI tool usage against union jurisdiction. If your commercial productions involve SAG-AFTRA talent, which most professional commercial productions do, the 2025 Commercials Contracts' AI training provisions are already in effect. Review your production agreements and vendor contracts for language that covers downstream use of performance data.</p>
<p>Second, understand the AI tools you're using and their training data provenance. Tools that use AI trained on real performers' data without licensing agreements are increasingly exposed to legal challenge. The vendors that are building proper licensing structures (the approach Suno took when it settled with Warner Music in November 2025) are positioning for a commercial landscape where proper rights clearance is enforceable, not optional.</p>
<p>Third, watch the 2026 negotiations closely. <em>(November 2026: they closed in June. The answer was a value test and arbitration instead of a royalty, as described in the section on the ratified terms above.)</em></p>
<p>The direction is clear regardless of how the specific negotiations resolve: AI use in union productions is becoming a structured, compensated activity rather than an unregulated cost reduction. Planning for that now puts you ahead of the adjustment rather than behind it.</p>
<h2>Producer Checklist Before You Use AI Talent on a Union Job</h2>
<p>Copy this into the pre production document of any job that touches SAG-AFTRA talent and any AI generation tool:</p>
<pre><code>1. Which contract covers this job? Commercials (2025) or TV/Theatrical (2026)?
2. Does any shot use a digital replica of a real performer?
   -> Signed Digital Replica Rider, with the exact uses listed.
3. Does any shot use a fully synthetic performer?
   -> Commercials: contact the union about the contribution before the shoot.
   -> TV/Theatrical: write down the "significant additional value" reason now,
      then give the union notice and negotiate terms before use, on the
      contract's timetable. The reason alone does not clear the shot.
4. Will any footage from this job train a voice clone or avatar model?
   -> Written SAG-AFTRA permission before any third party touches it.
5. Does every vendor contract (avatar, voice, edit house) repeat 2 to 4?
6. Who on the team signs off that 1 to 5 are done?</code></pre>
<p>None of this replaces a lawyer or the union's own guidance. It makes sure the questions get asked before the edit, when they are still cheap.</p>
