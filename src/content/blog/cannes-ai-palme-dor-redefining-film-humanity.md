<h2>What actually happened at Cannes</h2>

<p>When the 2026 Cannes Film Festival closed in late May, the Palme d'Or went to Fjord, a Norwegian drama about political polarization. Written by people, directed by people, performed by people. The more telling decision had been made before the festival opened: works principally generated by AI were excluded from the official competition, a rule that echoes positions taken by other institutions insisting on meaningful human creative involvement.</p>

<p>And yet, by every account from the Croisette, AI was everywhere else. It dominated the panels, the market, the private salons, the hallway arguments. Semafor described a divide running through the festival. The Hollywood Reporter profiled filmmakers who came to Cannes openly working with the tools and arguing that, used with intention and oversight, they expand what a small crew can put on screen. So the festival drew a line through its own building: the prize stayed human, and the conversation went to the machine.</p>

<p>My first reaction was relief. My second was suspicion, and the suspicion is the honest one. The question the festival is not asking out loud: what exactly does the exclusion protect, and for whom?</p>

<h2>The salon of 1859</h2>

<p>This has happened before, almost beat for beat. In 1859, photography was admitted to the Paris Salon for the first time, but in a separate section, apart from the paintings. Not art, the arrangement said. Adjacent to art. Charles Baudelaire, reviewing that Salon, wrote that if photography were allowed to supplement art in some of its functions, it would soon supplant or corrupt it altogether. He wanted the camera kept in its place: a servant of the arts, a keeper of records, never an author.</p>

<p>Photography did not supplant painting. It freed painting from the obligation of record, and painting answered with everything the camera could not do at the time: impressionism, abstraction, the visible hand of the person who made the image. The exclusion settled nothing. What settled the question was photographers making photographs, one deliberate picture at a time, until the institutions had no choice but to catch up. The Salon's separate section did not protect painting. Painting protected itself by finding what only painting could say.</p>

<p>A competition rule at Cannes is the same gesture: exclusion as a way of buying time while the real answer gets worked out somewhere else, by working filmmakers, on working productions, far from the red carpet.</p>

<h2>What this means if you make films outside the palace</h2>

<p>I have run a production company in São Paulo since 2012. The films I make are commercial work with small crews and tight budgets, which means the festival's rule does not touch me directly. What touches me, and anyone working at this scale, is what a rule like this sets in motion downstream. Institutions copy each other. Funds, festivals, broadcasters, and eventually clients will borrow this language.</p>

<p>A few practical observations for anyone working at that scale:</p>

<ul>
<li>Expect the question "what tools touched this film" to become standard on submission forms and delivery paperwork. Start keeping records now: which shots used generative tools, which model, what the human input was. It costs nothing today and answers an audit tomorrow.</li>
<li>"Meaningful human creative involvement" is a phrase lawyers will spend the next decade fighting over. The working version for a director is simpler: can you show the decisions you made? Treatments, shot lists, rejected takes, notes. The paper trail of judgment is becoming part of the film.</li>
<li>Read the rule carefully: it excludes works principally generated by AI. Almost nothing a working filmmaker makes is principally generated. The tools sit inside the pipeline, on specific shots and specific problems, not at the top of it. The rule, as written, is about a category of film most of us will never make.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Not a defense, a deferral</h2>

<p>Here is the part that gets missed in the coverage. The exclusion reads as a defense of human artistry, and it was reported that way. I do not think that is what it is.</p>

<p>Not because Cannes is wrong to draw a line. Because the line does not defend what people assume it defends. A competition rule protects a category: what may compete for the prize. It does not protect the actor whose likeness is being negotiated, the composer whose cues are being synthesized, the crew whose call sheet gets shorter. Those protections live in contracts and union agreements, in the fights that SAG-AFTRA and the WGA have been having in rooms with no photographers present. An awards rule is set dressing by comparison. Necessary, maybe. Sufficient, no.</p>

<p>And there is a quieter risk in the arrangement Cannes chose. When the official selection stays pure and the market downstairs sells the tools, the institution gets to have both: the prestige of the line and the business of crossing it. That is not hypocrisy exactly. It is what institutions do while a question is still open. But nobody working today should mistake it for an answer.</p>

<h2>The prize stayed human</h2>

<p>I do not know why the jury chose Fjord, and neither does anyone outside that room. What I know is what the choice says next to the rule: this festival, this year, decided that its highest honor names a chain of human decisions, from the first draft to the last cut. I think that instinct is right, and I think it is right for a reason the rule itself does not state.</p>

<p>A film is a long sequence of choices made by people who could have chosen otherwise, and who answer for it. A model can generate images all day. It cannot stand in the lobby afterward and be asked why. The festival can exclude the machine from the palmarès. It cannot excuse anyone, on any budget, from deciding what their film is for. That decision never belonged to the institution in the first place. It belongs to the person making the film, which is exactly where it has always been.</p>
