<p><strong>Update, November 3, 2026:</strong> this piece was first written in March, when Flow was the Veo 3.1 tool that had just absorbed Whisk and ImageFX. Three things changed since. At I/O on May 19 the product was renamed Google Flow and gained <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/ai/google-io-2026-all-our-announcements/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Gemini Omni Flash</a>, a model that takes text, images, audio and video as input and lets you edit a clip by talking to it. On August 27, <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-labs/new-creative-controls-google-flow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash</a> added start and end frames, cheap 360p drafts and export in 1080p or 4K. And Google AI Ultra dropped from $249.99 to <a href="https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/google-one/google-ai-subscriptions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">two tiers starting at $100 and $200</a>. The sections below are corrected where the March text stated something that is no longer true. This is a reading of Google's announcements, not a hands-on test.</p>

<h2>Three Tools That Used to Be Separate Are Now One</h2>
<p>In late February 2026, Google shipped a major redesign of Flow, its AI video creation platform. The change: they merged three previously separate products into a single interface.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flow:</strong> the Veo-powered video generation tool</li>
<li><strong>Whisk:</strong> the visual collage and mood board tool for combining reference images</li>
<li><strong>ImageFX:</strong> the text-to-image generator</li>
</ul>
<p>The result is a pipeline that goes from initial concept to generated image to animated, audio-synced video inside one workspace. For creators who were previously bouncing between tabs and exporting assets between tools, this is a meaningful quality-of-life change.</p>

<h2>What Veo 3.1 Adds in This Update</h2>
<p>The video generation in Flow runs on Veo 3.1, which Google updated alongside the Flow redesign. The practical additions worth knowing:</p>
<p><strong>Native audio generation.</strong> Veo 3.1 generates synchronized audio alongside video: natural dialogue, ambient sound, sound effects. In March that was the same pattern the industry was following with Sora 2 and Runway Gen-4.5; Sora has since been shut down, and native audio is now the default across every serious video model. The days of generating silent AI video and manually dubbing audio are ending.</p>
<p><strong>Native 9:16 support.</strong> The "Ingredients to Video" feature now generates natively in vertical format. For anyone producing content for YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, or TikTok, this matters. Previously you were cropping horizontal output or accepting quality loss. Now you generate vertical and it looks right.</p>
<p><strong>Cinematic style control.</strong> The model has improved understanding of cinematic language in prompts: references to shot types, lighting styles, and pacing that translate more accurately into output. Still imperfect, but noticeably better than earlier versions.</p>

<h2>The Whisk Integration Is the Most Interesting Part</h2>
<p>Whisk was always an underrated tool. The ability to combine multiple reference images into a visual direction (character appearance, environment, style) and generate from that combination is genuinely useful for pre-production work.</p>
<p>Now that Whisk feeds directly into Flow, you can build a visual reference board, generate still frames that match your direction, then animate those frames into video. That's a coherent pipeline for concept development that didn't exist cleanly before.</p>
<p>For pitching a visual direction to a client or collaborator before committing to production, this is practical. You can show a near-complete visual language at the concept stage without involving a designer or motion artist.</p>

<h2>Pricing: Where It Gets Complicated</h2>
<p>Flow is free to access with usage limits. The paid tiers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Google AI Plus:</strong> the entry plan, which since May 19 includes Gemini Omni in Flow</li>
<li><strong>Google AI Pro:</strong> $19.99/month, a monthly allowance of Flow credits, plus pay-as-you-go top-ups</li>
<li><strong>Google AI Ultra:</strong> two tiers since May 19, starting at $100/month and $200/month (the old $249.99 price is gone), with the highest allowances</li>
</ul>
<p>Credit costs per clip change by model and by resolution, and Google moves them often, so read the live numbers on the <a href="https://labs.google/flow/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Flow page</a> before you quote a job. The free tier is genuinely useful for exploration and low-volume work. The jump from Pro to Ultra is still steep, even at the new $100 floor. For professional use that requires consistent volume, the mid-tier is the right entry point. The Ultra tier is for teams or heavy production pipelines.</p>

<h2>How It Compared to Runway in March</h2>
<p><em>Dated note: this comparison describes March 2026. Runway is no longer part of my working stack; today the production work runs through Higgsfield, with Seedance and Kling inside it.</em></p>
<p>In March, Runway Gen-4.5 was the benchmark for professional-quality output where creative control mattered most. The model has more nuanced response to directorial prompts and the multi-shot editing that creates one-minute videos with character consistency is still ahead of what Flow produces.</p>
<p>Google Flow had the advantage of the integrated pipeline and the free entry point. For creators who are building a workflow from scratch and want everything in one place, it's a solid starting point. For productions where output quality is the primary concern, Runway is still the benchmark.</p>
<p>They solve slightly different problems. Flow is a creative workspace. Runway is a production tool. The distinction is real in practice.</p>

<h2>What I Would Actually Use This For</h2>
<p>The specific use case where Google Flow makes sense: early-stage concept development with clients who aren't technically sophisticated. The integrated Whisk-to-video pipeline lets you walk through a visual concept in a single session (mood board to generated frames to animated preview) without explaining three different tools.</p>
<p>That's a real workflow improvement for client-facing work. The output quality at that stage doesn't need to be final. It needs to communicate direction. Flow handles that well.</p>
<p>If you haven't explored it since the redesign, it's worth an afternoon. The integration is cleaner than it was six months ago and the Veo 3.1 output at native 9:16 is noticeably better for short-form content.</p>

<h2>How to use Google Flow to make videos, step by step</h2>
<p>This is the order I would follow for a concept or a pitch, which is the job Flow is built for. Each step comes from features Google has announced; check the labels in your own account, because Flow renames things between releases.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Open a project and set the frame first.</strong> Pick 16:9 or 9:16 before generating anything. Reframing later costs you the composition you approved.</li>
<li><strong>Build the reference set.</strong> Upload or generate the stills for character, location and style. This is the old Whisk job, now inside Flow. One approved frame per element beats ten adjectives in a prompt.</li>
<li><strong>Draft at 360p.</strong> Omni 1.1 Flash lets you test concepts at 360p for fewer credits. Burn the cheap drafts on blocking and timing, not on beauty.</li>
<li><strong>Lock the start and end frames.</strong> For any shot that has to connect to the next one, give the model both frames. That is the closest Flow comes to continuity.</li>
<li><strong>Fix by conversation, not by regeneration.</strong> Omni edits a clip from a sentence. Ask for the one change, the hand, the light, the speed, and keep the rest.</li>
<li><strong>Export only the approved shots in 1080p or 4K.</strong> High resolution is where the credits go. Pay for it once, on the shots that survived the review.</li>
</ol>
<p>A shot brief you can paste into Flow and adapt:</p>
<pre><code>Shot 03 of 08. Medium close-up, 50mm look, eye level.
Reference: frame_03_approved.png (character, wardrobe, light).
Action: she sets the cup down and looks out of frame left.
Camera: slow push in, ends on her eyes.
Light: single window key from frame right, soft, late afternoon.
Sound: room tone, cup on wood, distant street.
Duration: 6 seconds. Keep everything not listed exactly as the reference.</code></pre>

<h2>Is Google Flow good enough for a paid job?</h2>
<p>For the pitch, yes. For the delivery, it depends on what the client is paying for. Flow is fast at showing a direction to someone who has to approve it, and the start and end frames close part of the continuity gap that made March clips hard to cut together. What it does not give you is a pipeline you own. The platform picks the defaults, the credit price and the model of the month. If the job has to be re-rendered in six versions next week, keep the approved frames and the edit decisions outside Flow, so a change of price or model does not take the job with it. For the consistency side of that work, the prep matters more than the model, as I wrote in <a href="/blog/ai-character-consistency-prep-problem-not-model-problem/">the character consistency piece</a>. For what Omni costs when you push it to 4K, see <a href="/blog/gemini-omni-flash-4k-upscale-price-math/">the Gemini Omni price math</a>.</p>

<h2>The pattern I keep noticing across these unified launches</h2>
<p>Google Flow was the third "unified workspace" launch of the year. Adobe shipped a similar consolidation. Runway has been quietly merging its image and video editors. The arc is clear: the era of switching between five separate AI tools to make one piece is ending.</p>
<p>That is good for working creators. The cognitive overhead of context-switching between Whisk, ImageFX, Veo, Runway, and a non-AI editor was real. Hours per week, not minutes.</p>
<p>The tradeoff to keep an eye on is concentration. When the workspace is unified inside a single platform, the platform decides the defaults. The defaults shape the output. The output shapes the visual culture. Convenience always comes with that quiet cost. Worth using these tools, worth knowing they are not neutral.</p>
